Troubleshooting · Goeppertia / Calathea

Brown edges, prayer plants and Dutch tap water.

Crisp margins are a symptom, not a diagnosis. Start with roots, watering pattern and room conditions before changing everything.

Short answer

Brown edges on Calathea or Goeppertia can come from dry air, uneven moisture, damaged roots, excess fertiliser or mineral accumulation. Dutch tap-water hardness varies by supplier and location, so check your postcode’s water report and run one controlled change rather than assuming all tap water is the cause.

Is Dutch tap water too hard for Calathea?

There is no single Dutch hardness value. Waternet reports Amsterdam tap water around 7.8 °dH and describes the Dutch average as roughly 8 °dH; Vitens explains that hardness varies by location because it reflects calcium and magnesium in the source water. Those minerals are not automatically a plant problem.

Sensitive foliage can react to an accumulation of dissolved salts from water and fertiliser, particularly when evaporation is high or pots are only given small sips. If your local report shows higher mineral content and other causes are controlled, compare with rainwater or suitable low-mineral water for several new leaves. Do not expect damaged brown tissue to turn green again.

Drying patternPot becomes bone dry, then floodsAim for more even moisture without constant saturation
HeatingDamage worsens beside radiatorMove away from hot airflow and measure humidity

Build stable conditions

Give bright filtered light, a warm stable position and an airy mix that remains lightly moist without becoming stagnant. Goeppertia orbifolia is described by NC State Extension as preferring indirect sunlight and moist, well-drained soil. Keep it away from cold draughts and central-heating blasts.

Humidity is relative: warm radiator air can lower it quickly. Grouping plants may help a little, but a measured humidifier is more effective when a room is genuinely dry. Keep airflow and hygiene good; persistently wet leaves and stagnant air create other problems.

A fair two-week experiment

  1. Photograph the plant and mark the newest unfolding leaf.
  2. Confirm drainage and stop leaving water in the outer pot.
  3. Stabilise moisture and move away from direct radiator air.
  4. Check your water supplier’s postcode-level report.
  5. If needed, change only the water source for several weeks of new growth.
Judge new tissue. Trimming a brown edge is cosmetic. Success means the next leaves emerge with less damage while roots and growth remain healthy.
