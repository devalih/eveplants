Dutch tap water
for plants.

Hardness varies by location. Learn what it means, when it is worth changing your routine, and why water is rarely the only explanation for unhappy leaves.

Short answer

Dutch tap water is suitable for most houseplants. Hardness varies by supplier and location because it reflects calcium and magnesium in the source water. If a sensitive plant is struggling, first check light, root moisture, fertiliser and radiator air. Then use your local water report and test one change at a time.

What does water hardness mean?

Hardness describes the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium in water. It is not the same thing as whether water is safe to drink, and it is not a universal verdict on whether it is good or bad for plants. Waternet reports Amsterdam water at around 7.8 °dH and describes the Dutch average as roughly 8 °dH. Vitens explains that values vary by location and source.

Minerals can build up over time, especially in a pot that receives frequent small sips, strong fertiliser or high evaporation. But a plant with brown tips, pale leaves or slow growth may just as easily be responding to root stress, low winter light, inconsistent watering, direct radiator air or pests.

When is it worth changing the water?

Begin with the plant, not the trend. Sensitive foliage plants, plants in very small pots and plants that already show a white mineral crust can be reasonable candidates for a controlled test. So can homes in an area where a local report shows higher hardness. Do not switch every plant automatically, and do not expect existing brown tissue to become green again.

Good watering practice reduces buildup

Use a pot with drainage holes, water the root ball evenly, and empty the outer pot afterwards. A proper watering followed by a dry-down appropriate to the species is usually better than frequent tiny doses. Pause or reduce fertiliser when a plant is not actively growing, especially in a low-light Dutch winter.

Rainwater can be useful for plants that demonstrably respond well to it, provided it is collected and stored cleanly. If you use filtered or low-mineral water, make it one measured change, not a reason to ignore the rest of the care system.

Run a fair four-week test

  1. Photograph the plant and mark its newest unfolding leaf.
  2. Check drainage, root moisture and placement, then stabilise those first.
  3. Look up your postcode-level water report from the supplier.
  4. Use a lower-mineral water source for that plant only, while keeping light, feeding and watering pattern unchanged.
  5. Judge the next leaves and root health, not the old damage.
One change at a time. Changing water, pot, soil, fertiliser and position together makes it impossible to know what actually helped.
